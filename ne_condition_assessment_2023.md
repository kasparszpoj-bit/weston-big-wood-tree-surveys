**All four fail on exactly two things**, phrased identically each time:

> "the unit fails on **composition** due to the prevalence of ash die back
> disease. The unit also fails on **structure** due to a lack of standing
> deadwood."

All four were **downgraded from favourable** in this assessment.

## Why unit 3 is separate: answered directly

> "The area of W8 woodland in unit 3 is small and mostly along the public
> footpath, so only 2 sample points were carried out. **The rest of the unit
> comprises of steep limestone quarry.**"

Unit 3 is the quarry. Natural England themselves only managed 2 sample points
in it. That independently confirms the LIDAR result: all four planned survey
squares covering unit 3 contain rock faces of 73 to 82 degrees, and the unit
has no transects because it is largely not walkable woodland at all.

## Things this reveals that we did not know

### 1. There IS an earlier survey, from 2009

Referenced in three of the four units: "similar to those found in the previous
survey in 2009", "similar to those recorded in the 2009 survey", "As in 2009,
there is localised sycamore". **This answers the open question about whether a
baseline exists for comparison.** Worth asking Jenny whether AWT holds the
2009 data, because it would turn a snapshot into a trend.

### 2. The rare whitebeam is a single known tree, and it is at risk

Unit 2:

> "Favourable vascular plant assemblage - **Sorbus eminens** - The only
> individual known on the site (recorded in 1980/81) is still present. The
> tree is overly shaded by ash, however this shading will reduce in the short
> term as the ash die. Additionally, there is likely to be significant
> compaction to the root system due to being next to a busy path. A secondary
> path has also started to form close to the tree. The shading and compaction
> has been noted and a programme of works will be written into the next
> management plan to close off the emerging path and do a staged haloing of
> the tree."

This changes the whitebeam gap in our analysis. It is not that the rare Sorbus
cannot be assessed; it is that there is exactly **one known individual**, its
location is known, and there is a planned intervention. Our survey's two
generic "Whitebeam" records may or may not include it.

### 3. The other notified plants are still present

- **Purple gromwell** (*Lithospermum purpurocaeruleum*): "present in one
  locality as in 2009" (unit 1).
- **Herb-paris**: "continues to be present across the unit, comparison with
  historic maps suggests that herb-paris is as widely dispersed as it was in
  the 1980s" (unit 4).

### 4. Sycamore is a much bigger problem than our survey suggests

Unit 4:

> "there is localised sycamore in the unit which is now contributing to
> **15% of the canopy and 80% of the understorey cover at stop 3** (and forms
> about 30% of large and 25% of small trees)."

Our survey found 8 sycamore stems, 0.5% of records, and concluded the site
passes comfortably on non-natives. **That conclusion needs qualifying.**
Natural England measure *cover*, we measure *stem counts*, and a few large
sycamores can dominate cover while being few stems. Their sycamore is in unit
4, the north east, which is exactly where our Q17, Q19 and Q20 records sit, so
the two datasets agree on location and disagree on severity.

### 5. Deer are a competing explanation for the regeneration failure

Unit 4:

> "Some bark stripping on small trees, **no seedlings and few saplings at stop
> 6 indicates some localised deer activity**, though coppice regeneration was
> ok."

Our regeneration bottleneck analysis attributes the seedling to sapling
collapse to ash dieback. Browsing is a genuine alternative or contributing
cause and must be acknowledged. It also cuts the other way: if browsing were
the main driver it would suppress all palatable species, not ash specifically
at 912:1 while wych elm manages 2:1.

### 6. Natural England expected the seedlings to recruit

Unit 1:

> "**All age classes of trees are present.** Although fewer medium trees were
> recorded, there are **many seedlings and saplings which will hopefully be
> recruited over the next few decades.**"

This is the sentence our analysis most directly challenges. The assessor saw a
large seedling bank and read it as future canopy. Our data shows ash
converting seedlings to saplings at 912:1 and **not a single oak sapling on
the entire site**. If that holds, the recruitment the assessment assumes is
not happening, and the site's structural outlook is worse than the 2023
assessment implies. This is the strongest contribution the survey can make.

### 7. Only one part of the wood is actively managed

Unit 2:

> "This unit contains the ride which is managed on a rotation for a diverse
> sward and scrub structure and coppice coupe - **the only part of the
> woodland currently managed.**"

Unit 1: "The woodland is currently managed with little intervention."

### 8. Other pressures named

Recreational pressure with compaction from visitors and dogs off lead;
**mountain bikers creating earthworks** in unit 3; **honey fungus**
significant across the wood; and the observation that "many large ash trees
are faring badly at Weston Big Wood **in comparison to trees of a similar size
at other reserves**".

### 9. A new management plan is being written now

Repeated in all four units: "Avon Wildlife Trust will be writing a new
management plan considering methods to address these issues."

**The analysis is timely rather than retrospective.** Findings that speak to
deadwood, regeneration and sycamore have somewhere to go.

## The timing coincidence that matters

| | Dates |
|---|---|
| Natural England field visits | 15 July 2022 (units 1, 2, 4), 23 August 2022 (unit 3) |
| AWT tree survey | 12 May to 6 October 2022 |

**The two surveys are contemporaneous.** They describe the same wood in the
same season, which makes them directly comparable rather than a comparison
across a gap of years. That considerably strengthens any statement that
reconciles or challenges the assessment.

## What to do with this

1. **Frame the report against the two stated failure reasons.** Composition
   (ash dieback) and structure (standing deadwood) are what the site is
   judged on, and the survey measures both.
2. **Lead the deadwood finding with their words.** They say "a lack of
   standing deadwood"; we can say zero standing stems above 50 cm anywhere in
   0.40 ha, which is the quantified version of their qualitative judgement.
3. **Raise the recruitment question carefully.** The assessment assumes
   recruitment; our data questions it. That is a genuine contribution, but it
   should be offered as evidence rather than as a correction.
4. **Qualify the invasives conclusion** with the cover versus stem count
   distinction, and mention their unit 4 sycamore observation.
5. **Acknowledge deer** as an alternative explanation in the bottleneck
   section.
6. **Ask Jenny about the 2009 survey.** It is the single thing that would most
   improve the analysis.
